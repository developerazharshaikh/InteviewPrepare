How to use this map

At senior level, interviewers test whether you understand the mechanism, not whether you can recite the API. At lead and staff level, the same topic becomes a lens for trade-offs, blast radius, and the call you'd make under constraints. Each pillar is structured the same way so you can drill consistently.

Don't memorize answers — rehearse reasoning out loud. When you hit a question, say the naïve answer, then the failure mode, then what you'd actually ship and why. That arc is what gets scored.

Beyond the language

The Staff & Lead lens

System design in .NET

  • Scaling stateless APIs: horizontal scale + distributed cache, sticky-session pitfalls, why in-memory state breaks at N>1.
  • Resilience: timeouts, retries with jitter, circuit breakers, bulkheads (Polly), idempotency for safe retries.
  • Async messaging: queues vs service bus, outbox pattern, exactly-once is a myth — design for at-least-once + idempotent consumers.
  • Data: read/write split, CQRS when reads and writes diverge, eventual consistency you can defend to a PM.
  • Observability: structured logging, OpenTelemetry traces/metrics, correlation IDs across service hops.

Judgment & trade-offs

  • Frame every answer as "it depends, and here's on what" — latency vs throughput, consistency vs availability, build vs buy.
  • Quantify: "this saves 40ms p99 but adds a Redis dependency" beats "it's faster."
  • Know when not to optimize — premature abstraction and over-engineering are the staff-level red flags.
  • Technical debt as a ledger: name it, cost it, schedule it. Don't moralize about it.
  • Reversible vs one-way-door decisions — move fast on the former, deliberate on the latter.

Leadership & influence

  • Code review as mentorship: comment on the "why," distinguish blocking from nit, raise the team's bar without gatekeeping.
  • Driving consensus on architecture: write the ADR / design doc, surface options, let the team own the decision.
  • Mentoring: unblock without taking over; grow others' judgment, not dependence.
  • Influence without authority — the defining staff skill. Persuade with evidence and prototypes, not title.
  • Managing up: translate engineering risk into business risk leadership can act on.

Behavioral (STAR, leveled)

  • Pick stories with real ambiguity and scope — cross-team, multi-quarter, measurable outcome.
  • Situation · Task · Action · Result — but spend 60% on your action and the reasoning behind it.
  • Have a clean "a project failed" story: what you learned, what changed in how you work.
  • Conflict story: technical disagreement resolved with data and a path to "disagree & commit."
  • Quantify impact in the org's terms — revenue, latency, incidents avoided, engineers unblocked.

A two-week study cadence

Assuming you already write C# daily. Front-load the topics most likely to expose shallow knowledge under pressure.

Days 1–2Async & threading + memory/GC — the two pillars where senior candidates most often have confident-but-wrong models. Rebuild from first principles.
Days 3–4Collections/Generics + LINQ — drill Big-O and IEnumerable vs IQueryable until deferred execution is second nature.
Days 5–6EF Core + Performance/Caching — N+1, tracking, cache invalidation, and how to profile rather than guess.
Days 7–8DI + Middleware + Design patterns — lifetimes, pipeline order, and patterns as trade-offs, not trivia.
Days 9–10REST + Auth + Exceptions + Delegates/Events — API design judgment, token flows, and the failure-handling story.
Days 11–12System design reps — design a rate limiter, a URL shortener, a notification fan-out. Whiteboard out loud, end-to-end.
Days 13–14Behavioral + mock loops — record yourself, tighten STAR stories, and rehearse the "it depends, here's on what" reflex.
